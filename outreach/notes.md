## 1. The honesty pass

Everything on the concept that is not straightforwardly theirs, so you are never
caught out mid-conversation.

### Facts I could not verify — confirm before you rely on any of these

1. **Every price on the site.** Read from their inner pages on **12 Aug 2026**.
   IV drips $149–$199, HydraFacial $150/$175/$210, weight management
   $550/month, 16 ZO products $21–$248. **Their HydraFacial page was running a
   "50% OFF" promo popup when I gathered.** Re-check before you send.
2. **The hours.** Their site says Mon & Wed 9–1, Tue & Thu 9–5, closed Fri–Sun.
   **Fresha contradicts this** with Mon–Fri 9–4:30. I used their own site.
3. **The address suite.** Their site says 30497 Ste 200. **Yelp says 30495**,
   no suite.
4. **"Nicole" and "Erica"**, thanked in a patient review, are not on their team
   page. Do not name them.
5. **LegitScript certification.** Their seal is on their site; the verification
   lookup is behind a reCAPTCHA I would not complete. **Not published on the
   concept.** Do not repeat the claim for them.
6. **The founding year.** Not stated anywhere. Their domain was registered
   **10 December 2022**, which is suggestive, not proof. Not on the concept.
7. **Whether Anjmun Sharma, MD is actually in the Agoura Hills clinic.** His own
   bio says he practices in Colorado Springs. He is on the team page and on the
   concept, but do not imply he is in the room.

### Deliberately left off, and why

- **No star rating, no review count.** Three sources give three different
  numbers (4.8/53, 4.8/18, 5.0/10).
- **No before/after photos, no pound figures, no timeframes** — including the
  founder's own published "22 pounds in four months". FTC weight-loss
  advertising rules. This is the single most important restraint on the build
  and it is worth being able to explain in one sentence.
- **No HIPAA badge.** It is a graphic with no issuing body behind it.
- **No compounding pharmacy named**, though their site names one.
- **The contact form is deliberately limited** to name, phone, email and
  preferred time, with a visible "don't send medical details" notice. Their
  site advertises HIPAA compliance and a standard form relay has no business
  associate agreement. If they ask why the form is so short, that is the answer,
  and it is a point in your favour.

## 2. Objection prep

**"We already have someone doing our website."**

Very likely true. Their ~190 near-duplicate city pages are the signature of an
SEO contractor on retainer. Do not attack them.

> "That's good, you should keep whoever you're happy with. I'm not trying to
> replace anybody. I built this because I liked the business, and if it's
> useful, use it. If your person can build it, send them the link."

Then, only if they seem open, the real point: those city pages are a liability.
Google treats mass near-duplicate location pages as doorway content. That is a
genuinely valuable thing to know and it is worth more than the sale.

**"How much is this really going to cost me?"**

> "A thousand to take what you've seen, with your content and photos in it,
> live on your domain, handed over. That's it. If you want more pages or a
> deeper build it starts at $2,500 and I'd quote you properly. Maintenance is
> $99 a month and completely optional."

Do not discount below $1,000 on the first conversation. The price is already
the argument.

**"Why is it so cheap? What's the catch?"**

> "It's already built. I'm not starting from a blank page so I'm not going to
> charge as if I were. Nobody hired me for this, I did it because I enjoy the
> work, and I'd rather start a relationship than win a bidding war."

That is true, and it is the most persuasive thing in the whole package.

**"My nephew / brother-in-law / a friend does websites."**

Extremely common with a family-run practice, and Kim and Beth Barrus share a
surname, so treat it as likely.

> "Then he should have it. Genuinely. Send him the link, he can pull anything
> useful off it. If he ends up too busy, I'm here."

Never make them choose between you and family. You will lose, and you will look
bad losing.

**"We want it but can't pay it all at once."**

> "Half to start, half when it goes live. Or three payments across three months
> if that's easier."

They accept CareCredit for their own patients, so they already understand
staged payment. Do not offer this unprompted, but say yes readily.

**"Can you just do the maintenance without the build?"**

Say no, politely. Maintaining someone else's WordPress on FastComet shared
hosting, with 234 pages of programmatic SEO in it, is a support liability at
$99 a month.

**What happens if they say no**

Nothing. That is the whole point of the approach and it needs to be genuinely
true. Send the one follow-up in §4, then stop. Leave the concept live for a few
weeks in case they circle back, then take it down.

## 3. Deliverability

**What's in the email that could trip a filter:** almost nothing, by design.
No attachment, one link, no tracking pixel, no shortener, no image, no styled
HTML. Send it as plain text from a real personal address.

**Specific advice:**

- **Link the deck, don't attach it on the first send.** A 1.4MB PDF from an
  unknown sender to a medical practice's `info@` inbox is the single most
  spam-like thing you could do. Send the site link first; send the PDF only
  after they reply.
- **Do not use a link shortener.** Bare `https://` URL, one of them.
- **Do not put the link in the DM's first message.** Instagram filters cold DMs
  with links harder than plain text. Ask if they want it; send on reply.
- **Send from a personal address**, not `hello@` or `studio@`. A cold email
  from a role account reads as a campaign.
- **No read receipts, no open tracking.** If it comes up later that you tracked
  opens on a cold email to a medical practice, you have lost the relationship.
- **Their MX is Microsoft 365**, so it is Exchange Online Protection doing the
  filtering. It weights sender reputation and link count heavily; a one-link
  plain-text message from a real mailbox should land.

## 4. The follow-up

Send **once**, about seven to nine days later, Tuesday or Thursday morning.
Reply in the same thread so it carries context.

> Subject: (reply in the same thread, no new subject)
>
> Hi, just floating this back up in case it got buried. The concept's still at
> [LINK] if you want a look. No reply needed either way.
>
> [YOUR NAME]

That is the whole message. It is written to be easy to ignore, which is the
point.

**Then stop.** One email, one follow-up, done. If you sent the DM as well, that
plus one follow-up is the entire campaign. A third message to a business that
has not answered twice is not persistence, it is a nuisance, and in a town this
size that reputation travels.

## 5. What to photograph, if they say yes

This is the highest-value thing they can give you. Every image on their site is
small (nothing over 1440px, portraits 300–400px) and most of their homepage
imagery is commercial stock.

**Priority order, by where it lands on the site:**

1. **The ten staff portraits.** One session, same wall, same light, same
   framing. Their current ones are shot on different days against different
   backgrounds, which is why the concept applies a gold wash to unify them. Do
   this and the team section goes from good to the best thing on the page.
2. **A hero shot of the clinic itself.** Currently the hero is a keyed-out
   stock model. A real photograph of their room, or of a provider with a
   patient, would replace the one genuinely inauthentic image on the site.
3. **The IV lounge.** They describe it as "spacious, tranquil" and there is not
   one photograph of it anywhere. It is a selling point they are not selling.
4. **The building exterior and signage**, for the contact page and Google.
5. **Treatment-in-progress shots**: a HydraFacial underway, an IV drip running,
   the Evolt 360 body scanner. The scanner especially — it is concrete,
   unusual, and nobody photographs it.
6. **The ZO / Circadia shelf**, for the skincare page.

**What NOT to ask for:** before-and-after weight-loss photos. They have a
Before & After highlight on Instagram, so they may offer. Decline for the
website. FTC rules on weight-loss advertising make them a liability, and the
concept is deliberately built without them.

## 6. Should this be shown at all?

**Yes, with two real caveats.**

**It is a good candidate because:** they are small and locally owned, the
founder is reachable and is not a clinician, their existing site genuinely
underserves them, and the material is unusually strong — ten named staff,
reviews that thank people by name, a founder who was his own first patient.

**Caveat one: this is a regulated medical practice.** Everything you say in
writing about weight loss can be held against them, not just you. The concept is
built to that standard (no results claims, no pound figures, no before/afters, a
PHI-safe form). **Do not improvise around this on a call.** If they ask you to
add a testimonial with a weight figure, or a before/after, the answer is
"I'd rather not, and here's why" — it protects them.

**Caveat two, and read this one properly.** Their Instagram bio currently says
**"Sustainable results, guided with intention."** Their site says GLP-1s help
achieve "safe, sustainable, and lasting results." Those are results claims that
are already live under their own name. That is their exposure, not yours, and
**it is not your job to point it out in a cold email** — it would read as a
threat. But if you win the work, it is a conversation worth having early, and
it is a genuine reason to be their web person rather than someone cheaper.

**One more thing that is not a caveat but is worth knowing.** The previous
concept for this business (build 004) was based on an assessment that their
branding was "category-generic" with "no distinctive palette". That was wrong —
they own a gold lotus, a navy, a script wordmark and a serif, and fifteen
minutes of pixel-sampling proved it. If anyone ever asks whether you looked
properly before redesigning, the answer is in `input/brand.md`, with hex values
and sources. That file is your defence on a call.
